Secure drops: identity, encryption, and still filling a 10 GbE link

Every Mac gets its own identity key, which lasts 90 days and is then replaced. Every drop is encrypted and authenticated end to end. A Mac you have never met is paired with two 6-digit codes: each person reads theirs aloud and types the one they hear. After that it is remembered until either Mac's key runs out, and no other key can take its place, whatever name it uses.

This app is for Macs on the same network, so the people using it are usually in the same room or on a call. That is an asset most secure systems do not have: a second channel, a human voice, that a network attacker cannot forge. The attacker may well be in the room and hear every word; the design assumes so and loses nothing by it.

Draft 2 of this plan was reviewed adversarially and found unsafe to build from. Draft 5 was reviewed again by a different model, which found no break in the handshake or the pairing but withheld approval over expiry, resource limits, revocation and storage. This draft answers every finding of both; the review record lists them.

What has been built (23 Nov 2026)

Everything in this plan is implemented on the secure-drops branch as version 0.3.0, on one Mac. What that does and does not mean:

  • Tested: 135 automated tests. The handshake against a published Noise vector; every byte of a connection, every derived key, a record, the pairing codes and identity numbers against a second implementation written in Python from this page (scripts/vectors.py); each finding of both reviews as an attack; a man in the middle who is told both codes; expiry against a clock that is held still, set back, and rebooted; whole drops through a proxy that cuts, stalls and corrupts. scripts/mutate.py breaks 17 guards one at a time and each is noticed by a test (the first run missed four, which were tests that passed with or without their guard).
  • Run for real: the command line end to end on one Mac with two identities and the real Secure Enclave storage: refused while unpaired, paired with two codes after one deliberate slip, a drop accepted on the terminal and compared byte for byte, a decline, the unattended grant, a reset.
  • Measured: encrypted, over loopback with both ends on one Mac: 3.2 GB/s with 8 connections, 2.6 GB/s with one, 10,000 small files in 1.1 s. A 10 GbE link carries 1.25 GB/s.
  • Run between two Macs (MacBook Pro to MacBook Air, gigabit Ethernet, the command line at both ends): found and paired by Bonjour name; 6.29 GB at 895 Mbps and 5,100 files at 913 Mbps, the wire being the limit; killed and resumed; through 19 flipped bits, 37 cut connections and a 30 second outage; an unpaired identity refused; a retired key refused. All arrived identical.
  • Not done: the app itself has not run between two Macs. The speed gate has not been run (it needs a link faster than gigabit). No person has used any of the new windows. Phase 5's review of the code has not happened, and no professional cryptographer has looked at any of it.

Where building it changed the plan, the plan was changed: the arrival limit (500 a second, not 50), the hello's remaining (a duration, not a date), a random per-launch value in the Bonjour record, pairing on a connection of its own, and the fallback for a Mac with no Secure Enclave. Each is marked where it occurs.

Where we were (0.2.2)

After the security release, a Mac is safe from a hostile drop: writes are confined, consent means what was shown, received files are quarantined. What 0.2.2 does not have is any notion of who the other Mac is, or any privacy on the wire.

  • A name is a claim. Any Mac can announce itself as "macbookairm4". Only the IP address is real, and on a LAN that can be spoofed too.
  • Everything is readable. File names, sizes and contents cross the network in the clear.
  • A drop in progress can be interfered with by someone who can see the traffic: the token that lets a data connection attach is sent unprotected.
  • The checksum is not a signature. CRC-32 catches accidents. Anyone can compute a matching CRC for altered data.

What we take from Signal, and what we leave

Signal solves a harder problem than ours: messages to people who are offline, through servers it does not trust. Both Macs in a drop are online and talking directly, so the trust model carries over almost unchanged while the machinery gets much smaller.

For the channel itself the plan uses the Noise framework (pattern XX), which WhatsApp and WireGuard build on, and for the codes a short authentication string with a commitment, the method behind ZRTP voice calls and Bluetooth's numeric pairing. Both methods are published and analysed. What is ours is the way they are put together: the two-code derivation, the extra key taken from the handshake, the record format and the resume proof. Those are specified byte for byte below and are what the reviews and the test vectors are for.

The trust model in one page

  • Identity is a key. Each Mac has one. Names and addresses are labels shown for convenience, and nothing is ever decided by them.
  • Trust is made by people. Two Macs become paired when each person reads a 6-digit code aloud and types the one they hear. Nothing else creates trust: not a name, not having been seen before, not being on the same network.
  • The codes may be overheard. They describe a connection that already exists; they are not a password. Someone listening in the room learns nothing useful.
  • A pairing cannot start by itself. No code exists until a person at each Mac has asked for one.
  • Trust is remembered per key. A paired Mac that comes back with the same key needs nothing, under any name or address.
  • No key is for ever. Every key has an end date, 90 days after it was made unless its owner chose less, down to an hour. On that date the Mac makes a new one and the old one is refused everywhere. A pairing lasts until either Mac's key ends; then two people renew it with fresh codes. A key copied without anyone noticing stops being accepted on schedule.
  • A key you know is never displaced by a name. Another Mac using a familiar name is a new Mac with a warning on it. It cannot block, replace or borrow the standing of the one you paired with.
  • There is no "send anyway". Nothing is sent to, or shown from, a key that is not paired.
  • Encryption is unconditional. There is no plain mode and no setting for one. There is no compatibility with 0.2 at all: this is a new protocol that happens to share a name.
  • It works with no internet. An air-gapped LAN is a supported place to use this. There is no server, no account, no online check of a key and no dependence on a time server.
  • Pairing is not permission. A paired Mac's drop still has to be accepted, is still confined to its folder, and is still quarantined.

7. An impostor

Three different things can happen, and none sends anything.

  • It uses the name with its own key. It is the marked tile of screens 2 and 5, next to the real one. Using it needs codes read out by a person you can hear, and there is nobody to read them.
  • It takes over the real Mac's address. Your Mac asks whoever is there to prove the paired key before anything about the drop is said. It cannot, and the tile loses its mark.
  • It holds a stolen copy of an identity that has since been reset or replaced. It can prove that key, and that is exactly what gives it away: your Mac keeps every retired identity and refuses it, as a likely copy, never as a new Mac to pair with (screen 9).

9. Known Macs, and this Mac's own identity

In Settings. Every pairing is listed with how and when it was made and the date it is good until (the earlier of the two keys' end dates). How long this Mac's keys last is set here: 90 days, 30 days, a week, a day or an hour. A Mac can be renamed for yourself (names must differ), verified again, or forgotten. Resetting this Mac's identity is here too, with a plain statement of what it costs.

A reset is for when you fear the key was copied, so it has to end the old key's life everywhere, not only here. Four things make that so:

  • The other Macs are told. Before the old key is destroyed, this Mac uses it one last time to tell every paired Mac it can reach: "this identity is retired". Those Macs stop trusting it at that moment.
  • Nobody is forgotten. The Macs you were paired with stay listed under "Pair again", each marked as told or not reached, until it is paired again or you remove it.
  • A retired identity is never trusted again. Every Mac remembers the identities it has retired. If one turns up later, it is refused and reported as a likely copy.
  • Time finishes the job. A Mac that was not reached stops trusting the old key on the end date it recorded for it, 90 days at most after the key was made.

Identity

  • Each installation has one static X25519 key pair at a time, s, with the time it was made and the time it ends (see Key expiry).
  • What counts as the same key. An identity is the 32 bytes of the public key in canonical form: the top bit of the last byte clear and the value below 2255 − 19. Any other encoding is refused when it is read, before any lookup, tag or number is computed, and a key agreement whose result is all zeros ends the handshake. X25519 still has a handful of distinct canonical encodings that behave alike in a key agreement. To this protocol each is a different key: one of them presented by the holder of a retired key is an unknown key, which is New and needs two people to pair it, exactly like any fresh key that holder could have made. Retirement and expiry are claimed for the key as recorded, not for every equivalent.
  • Where it is kept. One file, ~/Library/Application Support/<bundle id>/trust, mode 0600, excluded from backups, holding both the identity and the Known Macs store so that they can never disagree after a crash. Beside it, device: a Secure Enclave P-256 key (the opaque form only that Mac's chip can use), a P-256 public key w, and this Mac's hardware id (by which files from another Mac are told from a failing enclave), all made once per installation. device is never rewritten: it survives key expiry and Reset Identity, which replace only s. The trust file is sealed with AES-256-GCM under KDF(ECDH(enclave key, w), "nimbus-drop trust v1", 32), with a fresh random nonce for every write and "nimbus-drop trust v1" ‖ schema version as associated data. Opening it takes one Secure Enclave operation per launch.
  • Every change is one atomic replacement: write the whole new file beside the old, fsync it, rename it over the old, fsync the folder. A change is not acted on, acknowledged to another Mac or shown as done until that has returned. A lock file (flock) keeps two processes of the same identity from writing at once.
  • Why not the Keychain (draft 2 said Keychain). The properties draft 2 claimed, "this device only" and "readable only by our code", belong to the data protection keychain, which needs an entitlement backed by a provisioning profile. Tested on both Macs on 22 Nov 2026 with a bare binary: the data protection keychain answers errSecMissingEntitlement (-34018), in a terminal and over ssh. The login keychain works in a terminal but refuses over ssh ("User interaction is not allowed", -25308), which is how the second Mac is driven in every LAN test; and it travels with Migration Assistant, which would put one identity on two Macs. The Secure Enclave wrap needs no entitlement and worked in both settings: made, reloaded and unwrapped in 4.6 ms on the MacBook Pro and 4.7 ms on the Air over ssh.
  • What that gives, exactly. A copy of the file on any other hardware cannot be opened (tested: the MacBook Pro's enclave key, copied to the Air, loads and then fails with "unable to compute shared secret"), so backups, migration and a stolen disk image do not carry the identity. Software running as you on this Mac can ask the Secure Enclave the same question the app does; that attacker was already out of scope.
  • When the trust file cannot be opened, the reason decides what happens. It is never silently replaced by an empty one, because that would also forget every retired and expired key.
    • Neither file exists: a first installation. Make both.
    • The enclave reports that device is not its own: the files came from other hardware (migration, a restored backup). Set them aside under a dated name, start fresh, say so once.
    • The enclave cannot be reached, or fails for another reason: temporary. Nothing is regenerated. Receiving is off and nothing that changes trust is possible until it works; the window says why.
    • The Mac has no Secure Enclave at all (a virtual machine, as the project's test runners are): the wrapping key is kept in device itself, protected by file permissions only, and the log says so. Added while building.
    • The file is there and will not open (damage, an interrupted disk): set it aside, untouched, and ask. The person may start fresh, and is told in so many words that the record of retired and expired identities is lost with it.
  • The development copy and each command-line --name have their own bundle id or folder, and so their own identities.
  • A Mac's key tag, for logs and lists, is the first 80 bits of SHA-256("nimbus-drop key tag v1" ‖ s.public) in Crockford base 32, shown as 7F3K-92QD-M4TX-81WC. It is never sent in a Bonjour record and never the basis for trust.
  • A Mac's identity number is 30 decimal digits: H0 = "nimbus-drop identity number v1" ‖ s.public; Hi = SHA-512(Hi−1 ‖ s.public) for i from 1 to 5,200; the first 30 bytes of H5200 are read as six 5-byte integers, each reduced modulo 100,000 and written as five digits with leading zeros. This is how Signal derives each half of a safety number. About 100 bits. It is verified by being entered into the other Mac and compared by the app, never by eye.
  • The Known Macs store holds, per paired public key: the name you gave it (unique among your Known Macs), names it has announced, when and how it was paired, the end date of its key, when last seen, the highest protocol major it has proved, and events. It also holds the retired list and the pair-again list. It lives in the trust file described above, so it cannot be edited, or read on other hardware.
  • Stated limit: putting back an older copy of the store, on the same Mac, is not detected. It could bring back a Mac you had forgotten. It can also bring back the standing of a key retired since that copy was made, until that key's end date; no key returns past its end date. P14 is claimed subject to this. It and it needs the access to your account that is out of scope.

Opening a connection, and versions

initiator →  "NDROP" ‖ count (u8) ‖ majors (u8 each, ascending)        e.g. 4E 44 52 4F 50 01 03
responder →  the same, with its own list                                 then the Noise handshake
data connection:  initiator → "NDATA" ‖ attach (below)
  • Protocol 2 is not spoken, answered or recognised (decided 22 Nov: it runs on four Macs, all the owner's, which will be updated together). A 0.2 Mac that connects is a stranger sending something unreadable and is closed. The only trace of the old protocol is that a Bonjour record saying v=2 is drawn as a dimmed "Needs an update" tile, from the record alone. The note in the project's own documentation that protocol 2's frame layout is "frozen for good" is withdrawn with this major.
  • These openings carry versions and nothing else. Before the handshake completes, nothing longer than 1 KB is read.
  • prologue = "nimbus-drop/3" ‖ initiator's opening ‖ responder's opening, byte for byte as sent. Both sides mix it into the handshake hash, so an opening altered in transit makes the handshake fail. The minor version is not in the prologue: 3.0 and 3.1 interoperate.
  • Both sides use the highest major in both lists. When a protocol 4 exists, removing it from a list in transit breaks the handshake. In addition the store remembers the highest major each paired key has proved, and refuses a lower one from that key afterwards.
  • The minor version, the app version and the name travel inside the encrypted hello that follows the handshake.

Discovery

  • The Bonjour record carries v=3, the name, and i: a random value made at each launch, by which a Mac leaves its own record out of its list (added while building; it identifies nothing and lasts no longer than the process). No key, no tag, nothing lasting. Bonjour says where; only a handshake says who.
  • Each address found is probed: the openings, then Noise messages 1 and 2, then close. After message 2 the responder has proved its static key and the prober has revealed nothing about itself. The tile's state comes from that key and from nothing else.
  • Probes run when Bonjour reports a result or a change, and the full handshake at the start of a drop proves the key again.
  • Results are kept per address, not merged by claimed identity. Two addresses that prove the same key are one Mac with two addresses; an address that announces a name and proves another key is its own tile. A record cannot move, capture or unmark a Paired tile.
  • When a drop reconnects, relocate offers every address that last proved the key, most recent first, and the reconnect handshake proves it again.
  • A probe is a stranger's connection and costs the responder a key generation and two key agreements.
  • Probing is rationed too, since forged Bonjour records could otherwise drive it: at most 4 probes at once, an address at most once in 30 seconds, results kept for at most 256 addresses.

  • Noise XX gives mutual authentication, forward secrecy and resistance to key-compromise impersonation, and hides the initiator's static key from everyone but the proved responder. The responder's static key is shown to anyone who initiates; that is accepted, and is what makes probing possible.
  • The initiator checks before it speaks. A sender aiming at a Paired tile compares the key proved in message 2 with the one it expects and closes on a difference, before sending its own static key or anything else.
  • Split, and one extension to it. Standard Noise ends with HKDF(ck, zerolen, 2), in Noise's own convention (ck as the HMAC key, an empty input, outputs chained with counter bytes 1, 2, 3), giving the two transport keys. This protocol calls it with 3 and keeps the third output, HMAC(temp_key, output2 ‖ 0x03), as master. The first two outputs are unchanged, so published Noise vectors test the handshake; they do not test master, which is ours and has its own vectors. The chaining key is then destroyed. Noise's HKDF is not the KDF helper defined at the top of this section; every later derivation uses the helper.
  • Handshake messages are framed like control frames (below) and carry empty payloads. Anything else in a handshake payload ends the connection.
  • purpose is drop, pair, verify, resume or retire. The responder refuses any purpose but pair and verify from a key that is not paired and within its dates.
  • Both sides must say paired: yes for a drop to proceed. If one side no longer has the pairing, the other is told ("it no longer has this Mac paired") and pairing runs; nothing is offered first.
  • A key on the retired list, or past the end date recorded for it, ends the connection as soon as it is proved. Completing the handshake proves possession of some key, not a place in Known Macs, and earns nothing by itself: see Admission.

The control channel

frame     = [u16 length][u16 length, bits inverted] ‖ Noise transport message (at most 65,535 bytes)
plaintext = [u8 type][u8 flags: bit 0 = more follows][u32 total length, first fragment only] ‖ bytes
  • The repeated length is there for accidents: a flipped bit in a bare length leaves the reader waiting for bytes that never come, which this project has already met once. A deliberate change is caught by the seal, and can do no more than stall a connection, which someone on the path can do anyway.
  • Large messages are fragmented (a manifest can be hundreds of megabytes; a Noise message cannot exceed 65,535 bytes). Fragments of one message are consecutive and in order; nothing else may come between them. Noise's own counter makes dropping or reordering one a failure to open.
  • Reassembly limits: one fragment (no reassembly at all) for anything before the receiver's person has accepted the drop; after, manifest and plan only, within the bounds under Admission and never more than 512 MB.
  • A control key seals at most 224 messages. A session that would exceed that reconnects, which gives the control channel new keys.

Pairing

Runs inside a Noise channel of its own (purpose pair), between the two static keys being paired; a drop then follows on a new connection. Used for a first pairing, a renewal, a replacement and a pairing again after a reset: there is one procedure.

S → R   pair-request {}                    R's person is asked. Nothing below exists until they choose "Pair…"
R → S   pair-go {}
S → R   commit  = SHA-256("nimbus-drop pair commit v1" ‖ h ‖ Ns)        Ns: 32 random bytes
R → S   Nr                                                               Nr: 32 random bytes, made after commit arrives
S → R   Ns                                 R checks it against commit; a mismatch ends the pairing

codes   = KDF(Ns ‖ Nr, "nimbus-drop pair codes v1" ‖ h, 16)
code_R  = (first 8 bytes as u64) mod 1,000,000      shown on R, typed on S
code_S  = (last 8 bytes as u64)  mod 1,000,000      shown on S, typed on R

each side: its person types the other's code; the app compares it with its own computation
           right  → send paired {}                  wrong → count it; a third ends the pairing with pair-failed {}
each side: stores the pairing when it has BOTH its own person's right code AND the other's paired {}

The transaction. A pairing is one transaction and every part of it is fixed once set: one connection and its h, one purpose, one commit, one Nr, one Ns, one pair of codes, one deadline, one window on screen, one key to be stored, and (where asked) one answer to "replaces or another Mac".

  • Each message is accepted exactly once and only in the order shown. A second commit, Nr or Ns, a message out of order, a message of any other type, or anything after pair-failed ends the transaction and the connection. There is no restart within a connection.
  • The codes are computed once, when the third value arrives, and never recomputed. The typing retries compare against those same codes. (An implementation that took a second Nr after Ns was revealed would let the other side search for a code of its choosing; that is the mistake this rule exists to prevent.)
  • A click or a typed code is acted on only if it comes from the window of the current transaction. Windows of ended transactions are closed, and a late event from one is dropped.
  • What is stored is the static key of this connection's handshake and the answers given in this transaction, nothing looked up again later.
  • A pending drop is never accepted by a pairing completing. Pairing ends, then the offer is made, then the person is asked.
  • Why hearing the codes does not help. Someone in the middle holds two different Noise sessions with two different h. To pass, the codes of the two sessions must be equal. In each session the attacker must fix its own random value before it learns the honest side's (the sender's is hidden inside the commitment; the receiver's is not made until the commitment has arrived), so it cannot steer either code, and cannot try again within a session. Its chance is one in a million against each Mac, and since each Mac decides independently the chance that at least one of the two stores the attacker's key is about two in a million per pairing. Two six-digit codes do not add up to a twelve-digit check. The codes are computed from values the attacker already sees; saying them aloud tells it nothing new.
  • Why two codes. Each Mac must learn from its own person that the codes agree. A message from the other Mac saying "it matched" could be written by the attacker. With one typed code, the Mac that only displayed it would have nothing but that message.
  • An attacker cannot make attempts by itself. Every pairing needs a person at each Mac to choose to pair and to type a code read out by a voice they know. Draft 2 let a stranger's connection start a code and spend guesses with nobody present.
  • One pairing at a time on each Mac, in either role. A request arriving meanwhile is answered busy. If two Macs ask each other at the same moment, the request from the lower static public key proceeds and the other is withdrawn.
  • Requests are rationed. One request panel on screen at a time, across all addresses. After "Not expecting this", a failed pairing, or a request abandoned by the other side, no new request is shown for 10 seconds, doubling each time up to 10 minutes, and resetting after a success. Every refusal, failure and abandonment is logged, counted and listed in Known Macs. Settings can turn requests off entirely, so that pairing starts only from this Mac.
  • Codes last 120 seconds from display and die with their connection. They are never logged.
  • A wrong entry by an honest person is a typing slip, so two retries are allowed; they are retries of typing, not new chances for an attacker, whose one chance was fixed when the values were exchanged.
  • If the last paired {} is lost, one Mac has stored the pairing and the other has not. The next handshake shows it (paired: yes against no) and pairing runs again. Nothing is sent on a one-sided pairing.
  • By identity number: purpose verify. The person enters the other Mac's 30 digits; the app compares them with the number of the key just proved, and on a match sends paired {}. The same rule applies: stored only when both sides have done it. Three wrong entries end it.
  • Replacement or another Mac. When the new key's name folds to that of a Known Mac, each person is asked which it is before the codes (screen 5). "Replaces" moves the old key to the retired list when the pairing is stored.
Changed from draft 2, which had the receiver invent a code that the sender typed into a password-authenticated key exchange (CPace). That protects only while the code is secret, and this plan reads it aloud in a room that may hold the attacker. The review showed a man in the middle within earshot pairing with both Macs and leaving no trace. Still rejected: codes from a hash of the keys with no commitment (the side that speaks last can search for a match), and a code used as a Noise pre-shared key (testable offline).

Key expiry

  • Every key has an end. A key ends its lifetime after it was made. The lifetime is this Mac's setting: 90 days by default, or 30 days, 1 week, 1 day or 1 hour. There is no setting for "never" (decision D8).
  • The end date is stated, and capped. Each side sends remaining in the encrypted hello: the seconds its key has left, so it is vouched for by the key it describes. (A duration and not a date, as built: two Macs on a network with no time server need not agree what day it is.) At pairing the other Mac records the earlier of that date and 90 days from now. A modified copy cannot claim a longer life than that, and a recorded date is never moved later.
  • A pairing lasts until either key ends. There is no separate clock for pairings.
  • After its end date a key is refused by every Mac that recorded one for it, exactly like a retired key, and cannot be paired again; the record of it is kept as a tombstone when the rest of its entry is dropped. The Mac that owned it has destroyed it by then: at the end date (or at the next launch after it) the app makes a new key, marks each of its Known Macs "renew" (keeping your name for it and its key, so that a later renewal can say it is the same Mac), and destroys the old key. Nothing is shown and nobody is asked: a marked Mac is renewed if and when a drop to or from it is next wanted, and its entry is dropped when its own key's end date passes unused. A drop in progress keeps its session, within the limit below.
  • Why this and not expiring pairings (draft 4 did that). Renewing a pairing with the same key renews trust in every copy of that key, a thief's included. Replacing the key is what ends an unnoticed copy. It also needs no message at the moment of change: both sides already know the date.
  • Telling a scheduled change from a surprise. An unknown key whose name folds to a Known Mac's is "expected" if that Mac's recorded end date has passed, and gets the wording of screen 10. Before that date it gets the full stop of screen 5. Both lead to the same codes; the wording grants nothing.
  • Renewal recognises what it can. The Mac whose key was replaced still holds the other's key in its marked entry. If the other Mac proves that same key, the renewal says "the same Mac you paired with before".
  • How time is counted. An end date is turned into a deadline in elapsed time when it is recorded, and a key is refused when either the elapsed time is used up or the calendar says the date has passed, whichever comes first.
    • While the Mac is up, elapsed time comes from the system's continuous clock, which runs through sleep and cannot be set. The trust file records, at each write and at least hourly, the boot session, the continuous time and the calendar time.
    • Same boot session at the next launch: the continuous clock gives the time since the last record exactly, whether or not the app was running.
    • After a reboot: elapsed time is advanced by the larger of the time since boot and the calendar time since the last record, never by less than zero.
    • So a calendar clock that is held still or set back, by a forged time server or otherwise, does not stop a deadline from arriving: every second the Mac is on is counted regardless. A clock set forward ends keys early, which costs a renewal.
    • Stated limit: time the Mac spends switched off is known only from the calendar. Someone who controls its clock across every period it is off can keep that off-time from being counted. They cannot stop the counting of time it is on.
  • What the limit of 90 days is measured from. Each Mac caps a key at 90 days from when it first trusted it. That is not the key's true age, which the Mac has no way to check: a thief presenting a copied key to a Mac that never knew it gets a fresh 90 days there, after a pairing with two people and codes. A deadline already recorded is never moved later.
  • Sessions end too. Expiry stops new drops at once. A session already authenticated may continue past its key's end by at most the shorter of 24 hours and that key's lifetime (so one hour for a one-hour key), then it is ended whatever its progress. Reset, retirement and Forget end sessions immediately instead (below).
  • The identity number and the key tag change with the key. An identity number is shown with the date it is good until.
  • With a one-hour lifetime nearly every drop begins with a pairing. That is the intended behaviour of that setting.

Data connections

master       = third output of the Split HKDF                       secret; one per drop, never replaced

stream_key   = KDF(master, "nimbus-drop stream v1" ‖ conn (u64), 32)

conn         = a number the sender takes from one counter for the whole drop, starting at 1,
               for every data connection it ever opens. Never reused, whatever happened to earlier ones.

receiver →   challenge: 16 random bytes, sent as soon as "NDATA" is read
attach       = session_id ‖ conn (u64) ‖ HMAC-SHA-256(attach_key, session_id ‖ conn ‖ challenge), first 16 bytes
record       = seal(header, counter n) ‖ seal(chunk, counter n+1, aad = header)
header       = file index (u32) ‖ chunk index (u32) ‖ length (u32)          28 bytes sealed
nonce        = 0x00000000 ‖ counter (u64); the counter starts at 0 on each connection and only rises
end marker   = a sealed header with file index 0xFFFFFFFF, chunk index 0 and length 0, and no chunk;
               nothing may follow it on the connection
  • No key and nonce pair can repeat. A key belongs to one conn, and a conn is used once. A connection replacing one that failed gets a new number and so a new key; draft 2 keyed by round and slot, and a replacement in the same slot would have started the same key again at zero.
  • The attach answers a challenge and is checked before anything is read or set aside. Because the tag covers 16 bytes the receiver has just made for this socket, a recorded attach is useless on any other socket, and the first connection to present a number is one that holds the key now. A wrong tag, an unknown session or a conn already claimed closes the connection; claiming a number is a single atomic step. Someone on the path can still delay or relay a connection, as they can any traffic; they cannot attach one of their own.
  • The header is sealed too, and opened before the chunk is read. An observer cannot see which file a record belongs to or where files begin and end, and a damaged length is caught at once instead of leaving the reader waiting.
  • A record must name an ordinary file of the manifest and a chunk that file has, and its length must equal exactly what the manifest implies for that chunk (the chunk size, or the remainder for a file's last chunk), as the receiver requires today. Anything else is a failed record. File and chunk counts must fit their fields, and are checked when the manifest is validated.
  • A record that fails to open is a damaged chunk, as today: that connection is closed, the chunk is asked for again, the failure is counted and logged as a warning, and the drop's summary reports the count. It never ends the drop by itself. Draft 2 ended a drop after three, which let anyone who had seen one session id stop a drop with three junk connections, and would have stopped the project's own chaos runs. A drop that makes no progress still ends by the existing rules.
  • A stream key has a budget: at most 238 bytes (256 GiB) and at most 224 seals, counting header and chunk seals separately. Before either would be passed the sender ends the connection with the end marker and opens another under the next number. Both figures are far inside the cipher's limits (Noise advises staying under 256 bytes per key). Counters are checked arithmetic; a counter that would pass its limit closes the connection and is never wrapped.
  • Opened bytes stay private until they are proved. A chunk is opened into a buffer nothing else can see, and only a chunk that opened is written or counted, whatever the decryption call leaves behind on failure.
  • The record format needs no lock shared between connections: each has its own key and counter.

Reconnecting and resuming

  • Reconnect within a drop (the 60-second window): a new connection and a new Noise XX handshake, purpose resume, which must prove the same static keys as before. Then resume {session_id, HMAC(resume_key, "resume" ‖ new h)}, answered with resumed {HMAC(resume_key, "resumed" ‖ new h)} and the plan.
  • The new handshake keys the control channel only. The master, the session id and the connection counter carry on unchanged, so data connections that outlived the control connection (this project has seen 5 GB cross that way) keep working, and a replacement opened during the reconnect uses the next number like any other.
  • How long keys live. The sender wipes everything when the drop ends. The receiver wipes the master and every stream key when the drop ends, and keeps only resume_key and the answer for the five minutes in which a sender that missed "complete" may ask again.
  • Resume after a failure (sending the same drop again later): a normal new drop with new keys. The partial folder's name is derived from the sender's static key and the manifest, so only the same paired Mac can continue it.

Withdrawing trust: retire, replace, forget, reset

Whenever a key stops being trusted on this Mac for any reason but its scheduled end, these steps happen in this order, and the change counts as made only when all have:

  1. The trust file is rewritten with the key retired (or forgotten) and the write has reached the disk.
  2. No new handshake, offer, pairing or attach is accepted from the key.
  3. Every pairing in progress and every offer from it waiting for an answer is cancelled and its window closed. An answer clicked after this point is dropped.
  4. Every session it authenticated is ended: control and data connections closed, and the master, attach, resume and stream keys wiped, including the resume key kept for five minutes after a finished drop.
  5. A drop being written is stopped before its files are put in place. Its partial folder remains for a later, properly paired resume. Files already delivered stay delivered; nothing can undo that.
  6. Only then is retired {} sent, where a notice asked for it, and that connection closed.

This applies when a notice arrives, when the person chooses "It Replaces…" or Forget, and to every Known Mac when this Mac resets its own identity. Scheduled expiry is different on purpose: there the session may run on for its bounded grace.

  • The retire notice. On a reset, before the old static key is destroyed, this Mac opens a handshake (purpose retire) with each paired Mac it can find, proving the old key, and sends retire {}. It waits for retired {} and records that the Mac was told. It tries for at most 10 seconds in all, then destroys the old key whatever the outcome. There is no later retry: the key that could prove the notice no longer exists.
  • A notice touches one entry. It is accepted only from a key that is paired, over a session that key has just proved, and it retires that key and nothing else. Nobody can retire a key they do not hold, and no notice can affect another Mac's pairing.
  • The notice names no successor. The new identity is never vouched for by the old one, which may be in someone else's hands. It earns trust the only way any does: the codes, or the identity numbers.
  • A thief can send the notice too. All that achieves is that the pairing goes on hold and two people pair again, which is the right outcome when a key has been copied, and less than the thief could do by using the key.
  • The retired list. Each Mac keeps the public keys it has retired: those named in a notice, and those replaced through "It Replaces…". Each entry holds the key, the name it was known by, the date and the reason. It outlives Forget.
  • A retired key is refused, not New. A handshake that proves a retired key is ended at that point. No request is shown and no pairing is possible. It is counted and logged in summary. The person is told at most once a day per retired key and three times a day in all, in a notice that does not take the keyboard; further attempts only add to the count. The notice gives the address and says that the name is what that Mac claimed, unverified.
  • The pair-again list. The resetting Mac moves every Known Mac to it: your name for it, its public key, and whether the notice was acknowledged. Nothing on the list is trusted. When one is paired again, the key it proves is compared with the one recorded: the same key is ticked off as "same Mac as before"; a different one is paired too (the codes are what count) and the list says its identity is also new.
  • What remains open, and for how long: a Mac that was not reached trusts the old key until it is paired again or the old key reaches the end date that Mac recorded, 90 days at most from when the key was made. The list shows it in red with that date.

Without the internet

A requirement (owner, 23 Nov): the system must work on a LAN with no internet access at all. What that rules in and out:

  • Time. No trusted time source may be assumed, which is why expiry counts elapsed time on the Mac's own continuous clock and treats the calendar only as a second way for a key to end sooner. Two Macs whose calendars disagree still agree closely enough: each counts its own deadline. A Mac whose calendar is far ahead ends keys early and says why.
  • Keys. Made, stored and checked locally. The Secure Enclave needs no network. There is no revocation list to fetch; retirement travels from Mac to Mac on the LAN.
  • Code. No cryptographic or other component is downloaded at run time. The app is notarised with its ticket stapled, so macOS can check it on first launch with no connection.
  • Updates. The daily check simply fails without a route and says nothing. An air-gapped Mac is updated by carrying the disk image to it. Since protocol versions must match to exchange drops, the Macs of an air-gapped LAN are updated together.
  • Discovery. Bonjour is link-local and needs no DNS server. Add by Address covers a LAN where multicast is filtered.
  • Tested as such. Phase 4's acceptance run is repeated with both Macs' internet access cut and their time servers unreachable.

The command line

  • --receive takes offers only from paired keys and asks in the terminal for each drop, showing what the app's panel shows; no answer is a refusal. It keeps the local-network rule. --pair runs a pairing in the terminal: it prints this side's code and reads the other's from standard input. --identity prints the identity number and the key tag; --known lists pairings.
  • Unattended receiving is a separate, explicit grant (the tests between two Macs need it): --receive DIR --unattended-from <key tag> [--max-bytes N]. It accepts without asking only from the one paired key named, only into that folder, one drop at a time, up to the byte limit (default 100 GB), for as long as the process runs and that key lasts. It cannot be combined with --any-network, is announced on the terminal when it starts, and has no equivalent in the app (decision D6). 0.2's "accept everything" is gone.
  • There is no flag that accepts unpaired drops, and completing a pairing never accepts a drop that was waiting. The tests build sessions from identities held in memory.

What is stored, and what is not

  • Stored: the device file and the trust file (the identity and the Known Macs store (with the retired list and, after a reset, the pair-again list)), history (file names and peers, as today), the debug log.
  • Never stored or logged: pairing codes, the random values behind them, session keys, the Noise chaining key. Logs use key tags.
  • Received data is written as plain files, as it must be.
  • What integrity covers. Encryption proves each chunk as it arrives. It does not prove bytes already sitting in a partial folder when a drop resumes: those are taken on the ledger's word and the file's length, as today, and the ledger does not survive a power cut. A resumed drop is therefore as trustworthy as this Mac's own disk between the two attempts. Proving it end to end would need a digest of every file, which is a pass over the data on each side; that is left for a later decision and measured before it is taken.

What stays visible on the network

The Bonjour record (a host name, and that Nimbus Drop runs), the two addresses, how many connections a drop uses, how many bytes each carries, and timing. Nothing is padded. Where one file ends and the next begins is no longer marked, but volume and timing still allow inference: a drop of one file shows roughly that file's size, and many small files look different from one large one. Anyone can also learn which key answers at an address by probing it, so a Mac can be followed from network to network by someone who looks. Hiding either is a different project.

Security properties claimed

Each is a claim a reviewer can try to break and a test can try to hold. Restated after each review. The first rated P4, P7, P10 and P11 of draft 2 as not achieved; the second gave a counterexample to P16 of draft 5 and asked for P1, P4, P9, P11, P12 and P14 to be narrowed to what is actually guaranteed.

P1
Confidentiality. File names, the folder tree and file contents are encrypted. Someone who sees all traffic of a drop sees the total volume, the number of connections and the timing, and may infer from those: nothing is padded.
P2
Integrity. Any change to any byte in transit is detected before that byte is written to disk: a chunk is opened whole, then written.
P3
Peer authentication. A drop completes only between the two static keys that were paired. No third key can take either role.
P4
Pairing soundness. Against an attacker who controls the network and hears everything said, a Mac stores a pairing with a key other than the one its person meant with probability at most 1 in 1,000,000 per pairing carried out; for at least one of the two Macs, about 2 in 1,000,000. A pairing is carried out only when a person at each Mac chose to pair and typed a code. The attacker cannot start one, repeat one or test anything offline. This holds for the exact-once transaction as specified and for no looser one.
P5
Forward secrecy. Compromise of both static keys after a drop does not reveal that drop.
P6
No impersonation by key theft of the victim. Stealing Mac A's static key does not let an attacker pose as B to A.
P7
A known key is never displaced. A paired entry's key is replaced only by a completed pairing in which this Mac's person chose "replaces". Nothing another key announces or sends changes whether a paired key can exchange drops, or where its tile points.
P8
No downgrade. Two protocol 3 Macs never exchange a drop unencrypted. When later majors exist, two Macs that both support one never complete a handshake on an earlier one because of anything changed in transit.
P9
Nothing before proof. A sender aiming at a Mac it knows reveals nothing about itself or the drop, its own key included, until the receiver has proved the expected key. In a first pairing the initiator's key is necessarily shown before any person has verified anything, and any Mac shows its key to whoever probes it. A receiver shows nothing from an unpaired key but a rationed request to pair.
P10
No nonce reuse. No key ever seals two different things with the same nonce. This follows from one counter per drop for connection numbers and one counter per connection for records, and from nothing else.
P11
Session binding. Only a holder of the session's keys at that moment can attach a data connection or resume a control connection; each connection number is claimed once; anyone else is turned away before a byte is buffered and cannot end the drop. Someone on the path can delay or relay, as with any traffic.
P12
Trust cannot be made or moved by copying files. The stored trust file opens only on the hardware that made it, and an edited one does not open. Not claimed: protection of the key in the running app's memory from software running as you, or detection of an older copy put back on the same Mac.
P13
0.2.2's defences are unchanged, and limits follow authorisation. Confined writes, offer and manifest agreement, quarantine and the local-network rule hold for paired Macs too. Every connection is held to the budget of its class (Admission); completing a handshake with a key nobody paired raises no limit.
P14
A retired identity is never trusted again. Once a Mac has durably retired a key, no handshake with that key as recorded leads to a drop, a request on screen or a pairing; sessions it had are ended and offers it had waiting are cancelled before the retirement is acknowledged. Forgetting the Mac does not undo this. Subject to the stated limits: an older trust file put back, a trust file lost to damage with the person's explicit consent, and equivalent encodings, which count as unknown keys.
P15
A reset transfers no trust and harms no one else. Nothing sent with an old identity can make another Mac trust a new one, and a retire notice affects only the entry of the key that proved it.
P16
No key is for ever. No Mac begins a drop or a pairing with a key after the deadline it recorded for it: at most 90 days of elapsed time from when that Mac first trusted the key, counted while the Mac is on whatever its calendar clock is told, and never extended by use or by anything the key's holder says. A session already running ends within the stated grace. Not claimed: the key's true age, or the counting of time a Mac spends switched off under someone who controls its clock.
P17
People are not asked by machines alone. A stranger on the network can put at most one request to pair on screen at a time, at a rate that falls with each refusal, and can cause no other panel.
P18
Withdrawn trust is withdrawn everywhere at once. After a retirement, replacement, Forget or Reset is reported as done, nothing authenticated earlier under the withdrawn key can still deliver, accept, attach or resume.
P19
Nothing depends on the internet. Pairing, drops, expiry, reset and recovery work identically on a network with no route out and no time server. The only outside contact is the update check, which is optional and whose failure changes nothing.

  • The cost at 10 GbE is small: about 15% of one core per side, spread over 8 connections. The slower Mac has more than five times the headroom needed on a single core.
  • The real limits are unchanged: reading from and writing to disk, and the copies between. The seal replaces the CRC pass, but where the CRC only read the chunk, sealing also writes it out, so it costs a pass over memory. CryptoKit's in-place seal and open exist only from macOS 27 (checked in the installed SDK); on macOS 15, our minimum, sealing returns a new buffer. The figures above were measured with that allocating call and are already several times what is needed, so no rule about allocation is imposed: the first task of Phase 3 is to measure the macOS 15 path inside the real pipeline, use the in-place calls where the system has them, and accept a copy wherever the gate still holds. Meeting the gate is never a reason to write our own GCM or to use a private API.
  • The handshake costs nothing that shows: under a millisecond per drop, and about 5 ms once per launch to open the identity.
Not yet measured, and it must be: the engine has never run on a real link faster than gigabit. The 25.6 Gbit/s figure is loopback, one Mac doing both jobs. Both Macs have Thunderbolt, and a Thunderbolt cable between them is a network link well above 10 Gbit/s. That is the test rig, with nothing to buy. Phase 0 measures the current engine on it, before any cryptography, so the starting point is known.

The gate, in file bytes delivered (goodput), over the whole path from one disk to the other, not the cipher alone. A 10 Gbit/s link cannot carry 10 Gbit/s of file bytes once packet headers are paid for: with standard frames the ceiling is about 9.4. So: (1) on the same link at the same commit, an encrypted drop delivers at least 95% of what an unencrypted build of the same engine delivers; and (2) on a link held to 10 Gbit/s, an encrypted drop delivers at least 9.0 Gbit/s of file bytes. The figure on the open Thunderbolt link, where memory is the limit, is recorded for each phase but does not gate it.

Anti-fragile: built so that trouble makes it stronger

The project already works this way: a pulled cable became a chaos test, and the security review became fifteen attack tests. The plan extends that to cryptography, where failures are quieter.

  • Fail closed, never fall back. No plain mode, no negotiation. The only answer to trouble is to stop and say so.
  • Misuse ruled out by structure. One small component hands out keys and counters and cannot issue the same pair twice. No other code touches a key.
  • An attacker in the test suite. The chaos proxy learns to be an active man in the middle: substitute keys, run the pairing from the middle with both codes known, replay and reorder records, replay whole connections, race an observed attach, send each pairing message twice and out of order, flood with completed handshakes from fresh keys, retire a key mid-drop, splice one drop's connections into another, strip or truncate the handshake, replay a pairing, flip bits. Every case must end in a clean refusal with the right message.
  • Known answers. The handshake is checked against published Noise test vectors. Everything of our own (the master, every derived key, records, both pairing codes, the attach and resume proofs, the identity number) is checked against vectors produced by a second implementation written independently of the app's, in another language, so "it talks to itself" is never mistaken for "it is correct".
  • Every finding becomes a test first. Reproduce it, watch the test fail, fix it, keep the test, and check the test fails again with the fix removed.
  • Attacks are noticed and counted. A failed proof, a wrong code, a refused request to pair, a retired key, a record that will not open: each is logged at warning or error level, shown in Internals, and kept in Known Macs against the name involved. A Mac under attack looks different from one that is not.
  • Time and storage are attacked too. Tests run expiry against a frozen clock, repeated rollback, sleep, restart, reboot and forward jumps, and kill the process at every step of every write to the trust file.
  • Recovery is a designed path. Reset identity, forget a Mac, pair again, renew after a key ends. None needs a support call or a file edited by hand.
  • Layers stay. Everything 0.2.2 added applies to paired Macs. Encryption protects the wire; it does not vouch for the other end.
  • Small surface. Standard methods in a composition of our own, small enough to specify byte for byte. The primitives come from the operating system. Noise is implemented here because it is a few hundred lines over those primitives, with public vectors; that is a judgement about audit burden, not a rule, and a mature Noise library is the fallback if review of ours finds reason.

Decisions

Three are settled. The rest carry my recommendation and are yours to change.

D1

How is the code used? decided 22 Nov

Draft 2 recommended one secret code typed into a password-authenticated exchange. The review showed that fails when the attacker can hear, which in one room is the normal case.

Decided: two codes derived from the connection behind a commitment, each read aloud and typed on the other Mac. Nothing depends on the codes being secret, nothing can be clicked through, and no outside library is needed.
D2

Where does the identity key live?

The Keychain properties draft 2 relied on need a provisioning profile and are unavailable to the command line, the tests and ssh sessions (tested on both Macs). A plain file would migrate to a new Mac and give two Macs one identity.

Recommend: a file that only this Mac's Secure Enclave can open, as specified. Device-bound by hardware, no entitlement, identical for app and command line. The alternative is to add a provisioning profile to the build and keep a separate, weaker store for the command line.
D3

Is there any drop without pairing?

Recommend: no. Pairing takes half a minute, the people can hear each other, and it removes a whole state from the design.
D4

What happens to 0.2 Macs? decided 22 Nov

Decided: nothing. Protocol 2 is not honoured in any way; it runs on four Macs you control and they will be updated together. Protocol 3 does not read, answer or recognise it.
D5

May an unknown key ever be waved through?

Recommend: never. There is no "send anyway" anywhere; the only way to exchange anything with a key is to pair with it.
D6

Should a paired Mac be able to skip the accept panel, or reach you from another network?

Recommend: neither in the app in 0.3. Both later, per Mac, off by default. The command line gets one narrow exception, --unattended-from, because testing between two Macs needs a receiver nobody is sitting at: one named key, one folder, a byte limit, and only while that process runs.
D7

Post-quantum key agreement?

Recommend: not now. The protocol major leaves room; revisit when the minimum macOS allows.
D8

How long does trust last? decided 22 Nov

Draft 4 expired pairings and kept the key. That does not end an unnoticed copy of a key, since each renewal trusts the same key again.

Decided: the key itself expires. 90 days by default, with 30 days, a week, a day and an hour as the other choices, and no "never". A pairing lasts until either Mac's key ends. Each pair of Macs renews about twice in 90 days.
D9

Keep the retire notice? decided 22 Nov

You asked whether a revocation message could be turned against everyone else. As specified it cannot: it is accepted only from the key it retires, over a session that key has proved, and touches that one entry.

Decided: keep it, alongside key expiry. The notice acts at once when you know a key was copied; expiry acts when you do not.
D10

No internet required decided 23 Nov

Decided: everything works on an air-gapped LAN. No trusted time source, no online check and no download is assumed anywhere; the update check is the one optional outside contact.
D11

Should a resumed drop be proved end to end?

Encryption proves chunks in transit, not bytes left in a partial folder between attempts. A per-file digest would close that, at the cost of one more pass over the data on each side.

Recommend: not in 0.3; state the narrower guarantee, as the plan now does. Measure the cost in Phase 0 and decide with the number.

For reviewers

This plan is meant to be attacked before anything is built. Nothing here exists in code. The current app (0.2.2, protocol 2.2) is unencrypted and unauthenticated; its source and its own security notes are in the repository. This is draft 6, after two reviews; admission, elapsed-time expiry, withdrawal of trust, the trust file and its recovery, the attach challenge, key expiry, the pairing, the key schedule, discovery, versioning and identity storage are all new since the first review and have not been reviewed.

What to assume

  • Apple silicon Macs, macOS 15 or later, on one local network. A network attacker can read, drop, delay, inject and modify anything, and can announce any Bonjour record.
  • The people pairing can hear each other, in a room or on a call. The attacker can hear them too, but cannot speak in their voices.
  • Users are not experts and are sometimes in a hurry.
  • Throughput of 10 Gbit/s between two Macs is a hard requirement.

What to try to break

  1. Each of P1 to P19. Is the claim true of the specification as written? What is under-specified?
  2. The pairing: the commitment, the order of the three values, two codes from one derivation, the rule for storing. Can someone in the middle who hears both codes, and controls timing, get either Mac to store the wrong key with better than the stated chance? Can the two retries of typing be turned into more chances?
  3. The request to pair: can it still be used to wear a person down or to slip in beside a genuine pairing in progress?
  4. A familiar name on an unknown key: the folding of names, the two choices, and what a hurried person does with them. Is "It Replaces…" too easy to choose for an attacker's Mac?
  5. The data records: connection numbering under replacement and reconnect, the authenticated attach, sealed headers, the end marker.
  6. Reconnect: the two proofs, and a master that outlives its control channel.
  7. Versions: the openings in the prologue. Is there any way to make two Macs settle on less than they both support?
  8. Discovery by probe: what a flood of records or probes costs, and whether per-address results can still mislead.
  9. Identity storage: is the Secure Enclave wrap what it is said to be? What happens on restore, migration, a second user account, a Mac without a working enclave?
  10. Key expiry and the clock: can any key be accepted after its date? Does the gentler wording for an expected new key give an impostor who waits for the date anything but wording? Can forced early expiry be used to train people to pair carelessly?
  11. Reset and retirement: can a thief of the old key gain anything, or deny the owner the reset?
  12. Performance: does anything in the record format or key schedule prevent sealing in place at 1.25 GB/s?
  13. Admission: is there still a state a stranger can hold open, or work it can cause, outside a counted class?
  14. Elapsed time: the rules for restart and reboot, and whether the stated limit for time spent off is the only one.
  15. Withdrawal of trust: any path by which something authenticated before a retirement acts after it.
  16. The trust file: crash at each step, the five recovery cases, two processes at once.
  17. What is missing entirely.

What a finding should contain

The property or section it concerns, a concrete sequence of events that shows the problem, how serious it is and why, and a proposed change if there is one. "This is fine" with reasons is a finding too.
